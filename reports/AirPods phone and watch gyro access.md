# Aircade turned an AirPod into a Wiimote

Hack the North 2026 had no single winner. Its top award went to **12 equal finalists** chosen from 349 submissions. The AirPods "Wii remote" you remember is **Aircade**, built by Adyan Ullah, Yegor Zaytsev and Farhad Sadig. It won one of three **OpenAI: API Prizes** but was not a finalist. Its tagline claims the team "hacked the apple airpods SDK", but the code uses Apple's **public `CMHeadphoneMotionManager` API on a Mac**. The player turns off Automatic Ear Detection and holds one earbud like a Wii Remote. A native Swift arcade then turns the earbud's orientation into a menu pointer, a tennis racket and a light saber. The orientation data arrives about 30 to 50 times a second, in uneven bursts. That pipeline runs only on Apple hardware, so on Windows 11 the phone is the easiest motion sensor to use. Any iPhone or Android browser streams **about 60 Hz** accelerometer and gyroscope data over HTTPS with nothing installed. Native code reaches **100 to 200 Hz**. Free apps such as phyphox, Sensor Logger and SensorServer send data to a laptop without any code on the phone. Apple Watch motion (100 Hz live) has to **relay through an iPhone** or an internet server, because watchOS blocks direct network sockets and local-network requests. AirPods motion likewise needs an iPhone, iPad or Mac in the chain, except through experimental drivers. Fitbit is effectively closed: only the discontinued Ionic, Versa and Sense ever let apps read a gyroscope, third-party installs end on **November 28, 2026**, and Google's replacement cloud API has no raw motion data. For the BioHacks ALS project, a browser-based walking test can be built now, but the defensible claim is narrow. A phone worn at the waist measures steps, cadence and stride time well. Gait speed over a measured course is the measure with published ALS reliability (test-retest ICC 0.93, where 1.0 is perfect agreement between repeat tests). This research found no phone, watch or earbud test validated for catching undiagnosed ALS.

## Aircade ran on a public API and a settings toggle

### Turning off ear detection freed the earbud from the ear

`CMHeadphoneMotionManager` is Apple's only general-purpose public API for AirPods motion. Apple lists it for iOS, iPadOS, Mac Catalyst and macOS 14 or later, plus watchOS ([Apple: CMHeadphoneMotionManager](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/coremotion/cmheadphonemotionmanager)). It works with headphones that support Spatial Audio head tracking: AirPods 3, 4 and 5, AirPods Pro and AirPods Max ([Apple Support](https://support.apple.com/guide/airpods/control-spatial-audio-and-head-tracking-dev00eb7e0a3/web)). Each sample is a fused `CMDeviceMotion` record with four parts: attitude (the bud's 3D orientation) as a quaternion or as roll, pitch and yaw in radians; bias-corrected rotation rate in rad/s; gravity and user acceleration in g; and a `sensorLocation` naming the left or right bud. There is no setting for the update rate and no raw gyroscope data. An app without the `NSMotionUsageDescription` key in its Info.plist crashes when updates start (same Apple page). Aircade's `MotionModel` starts connection-status updates, then device-motion updates on a dedicated serial queue. It reads each sample's quaternion, rotation rate, user acceleration, timestamp and bud location ([MotionModel.swift](https://github.com/apollo-ullah/Aircade/blob/0b165b4/Sources/Aircade/MotionModel.swift#L254-L280)). A lock-protected buffer "coalesces sensor input so a busy UI consumes the newest reading, never a backlog", and a 60 Hz timer reads it ([LatestInputBuffer.swift](https://github.com/apollo-ullah/Aircade/blob/0b165b4/Sources/MotionCore/LatestInputBuffer.swift#L3-L5)). That structure came from a hard lesson. An early build "received zero motion samples", and the team fixed it by moving the callbacks off the main queue and creating a fresh manager on every reconnect ([VERIFICATION.md](https://github.com/apollo-ullah/Aircade/blob/0b165b4/VERIFICATION.md#L61)).

The real "hack" is a setting. Apple's WWDC23 session explains that with Automatic Ear Detection on, "you'll get a disconnect event when the buds are taken out of ear". It also says that "motion data is delivered to you from one bud at a time" ([WWDC23 session 10179](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2023/10179/)). So Aircade tells players to "Turn off Automatic Ear Detection, hold the earbud in your calibrated grip, and click Start AirPods" ([DEVELOPMENT.md](https://github.com/apollo-ullah/Aircade/blob/0b165b4/DEVELOPMENT.md#L64)). Apple offers no way to choose which bud streams. The app therefore locks onto the first bud it sees and requires an explicit handover when macOS switches. The team added this rule after a test where the player held the right AirPod but the logs showed the left one streaming ([VERIFICATION.md](https://github.com/apollo-ullah/Aircade/blob/0b165b4/VERIFICATION.md#L69-L75)). The single stream also shaped multiplayer: "The public headphone API also only gives us one active AirPods motion stream at a time, so our two-player mode pairs an AirPod with an iPhone" ([Aircade on Devpost](https://devpost.com/software/aircade-m4n6zv)). One packaging trap matters to anyone copying the approach. macOS refused motion access when the bare executable was launched directly ([VERIFICATION.md](https://github.com/apollo-ullah/Aircade/blob/0b165b4/VERIFICATION.md#L21)). The build script therefore wraps the Swift binary in an ad-hoc-signed `.app` bundle that carries the usage key ([build.sh](https://github.com/apollo-ullah/Aircade/blob/main/scripts/build.sh)).

### About 30 samples a second arrive in bursts

Apple publishes no sample rate for headphone motion, so the team measured it. Their logs record "approximately 50 Hz" early on and about 33 Hz later ([VERIFICATION.md](https://github.com/apollo-ullah/Aircade/blob/0b165b4/VERIFICATION.md)). They also recorded a resting left AirPod for 18 seconds and got **552 samples, or 30.7 per second**. Each sample's timestamp falls on a 20 ms grid, but many grid slots were empty: the gaps between consecutive timestamps were 20 ms (255 times), 40 ms (250 times), 60 ms (40), 80 ms (5) and 100 ms (once). The median delay from sensor timestamp to receipt in the app was 23.9 ms ([RecordedSensorCapture.json](https://github.com/apollo-ullah/Aircade/blob/0b165b4/docs/evidence/RecordedSensorCapture.json)). That 23.9 ms was computed from the recording, not reported by the app, and it is not end-to-end latency. It excludes rendering, and Apple does not document whether the sensor timestamp is set before or after the Bluetooth hop. The team's own docs say their sample-age figures are "not Bluetooth or motion-to-photon latency" ([DEVELOPMENT.md](https://github.com/apollo-ullah/Aircade/blob/0b165b4/DEVELOPMENT.md#L144-L152)). Poddle saw the same pattern: samples "arrive in pairs every ~40 ms, with gaps up to 78 ms, even though the sensor clock ticks evenly at 20 ms" ([Poddle NOTES.md](https://github.com/danielrltan/poddle/blob/7b0b37ada91f7d079e60a8e16ef844d42831877c/NOTES.md)). The receiving device matters too. Researchers measured **25 Hz** from AirPods Pro 2 on an iPhone running iOS 18. Losses were worst in noise-cancellation and transparency modes and exceeded 30% at 15 m through walls ([TWSCardio, arXiv 2501.06744](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.06744)). A small open-source tool reports a steady 50 Hz from the same model on macOS 26 ([airpod-pose-estimate](https://github.com/changtimwu/airpod-pose-estimate)). Aircade's code is built around this jitter. It discards samples older than 250 ms, pauses the game clock after 250 ms without motion, and requires re-centering after a full second. The team settled on those thresholds after a 280.1 ms gap following a hit tripped their original single pause rule ([DEVELOPMENT.md](https://github.com/apollo-ullah/Aircade/blob/0b165b4/DEVELOPMENT.md#L73); [VERIFICATION.md](https://github.com/apollo-ullah/Aircade/blob/0b165b4/VERIFICATION.md#L121-L123)).

### Geometry, not gesture recognition, decides every hit

Aircade treats the earbud purely as an orientation sensor. Calibration captures three poses: upright, tilted left, and tipped toward the screen. From these it builds a grip basis, a correction for how the player holds that bud, and saves one per bud. Every later quaternion is measured relative to that reference and smoothed with a 45 ms time constant ([SimpleGripCalibration.swift](https://github.com/apollo-ullah/Aircade/blob/0b165b4/Sources/MotionCore/SimpleGripCalibration.swift#L13-L47); [Tracking.swift](https://github.com/apollo-ullah/Aircade/blob/0b165b4/Sources/MotionCore/Tracking.swift#L4-L23)). The Wii-style menu pointer follows the earbud's "up" axis within a cone of ±22.5° horizontally and ±13° vertically. A 0.35° dead zone absorbs hand tremor. The earbud has no usable button, so you select by dwelling: hold the pointer over a target for one second ([Pointer.swift](https://github.com/apollo-ullah/Aircade/blob/0b165b4/Sources/MotionCore/Pointer.swift#L4-L54); [ControllerMenu.swift](https://github.com/apollo-ullah/Aircade/blob/0b165b4/Sources/Aircade/ControllerMenu.swift#L16-L87)). The swing detector fires at 3 rad/s, but it only flashes the saber blade. Points come from sweeping the virtual racket or blade between consecutive poses. A tennis ball must actually intersect an elliptical racket face. The 2.35-unit saber blade is checked every 0.035 units along its path, so fast strokes cannot pass through a target without hitting it ([DEVELOPMENT.md](https://github.com/apollo-ullah/Aircade/blob/0b165b4/DEVELOPMENT.md#L134-L142); [RacketGeometry.swift](https://github.com/apollo-ullah/Aircade/blob/0b165b4/Sources/MotionCore/RacketGeometry.swift#L34-L47); [Combat.swift](https://github.com/apollo-ullah/Aircade/blob/0b165b4/Sources/MotionCore/Combat.swift#L24-L60)). In the Devpost write-up's words, "Your racket still has to physically intersect with the ball for the hit to count." Acceleration "is shown/logged, never integrated into position". Instead, an optional webcam mode uses Apple Vision's hand-pose detector to supply hand position, much as the Wii's sensor bar did ([DEVELOPMENT.md](https://github.com/apollo-ullah/Aircade/blob/0b165b4/DEVELOPMENT.md#L109-L142)). To check for drift, the team compared the orientation changes the API reported with changes computed from the gyroscope over the same samples. The median ratios were 0.996 to 0.999, so they kept manual re-centering rather than adding a correction factor (same source).

### An iPhone and a local AI station complete the arcade

The second controller is an iPhone app. It runs Apple's `CMMotionManager` at 60 Hz and sends only the phone's orientation quaternion. The Mac advertises a Bonjour service, `_aircade._tcp`, and the phone connects over plain TCP with Nagle's algorithm turned off (TCP_NODELAY), so small packets go out immediately. Each JSON message starts with a 4-byte length field. The phone answers each request from the Mac with exactly one sample, "so there is never an unbounded queue of 60 Hz motion packets". The Mac discards any sample whose round trip reached 250 ms ([ControllerLink.swift](https://github.com/apollo-ullah/Aircade/blob/0b165b4/Sources/ControllerLink/ControllerLink.swift#L27-L88); [PhoneControllerHost.swift](https://github.com/apollo-ullah/Aircade/blob/0b165b4/Sources/Aircade/PhoneControllerHost.swift#L82-L135)). Motion data never leaves the Mac. Only game state goes to a local Node.js and MongoDB "station" that holds the API keys. The station sends the game state and five legal returns to GPT-6 Astra through the OpenAI Responses API, which picks a shot. The alternatives are Jev, a model reached through the Vercel AI Gateway, and a logistic-regression opponent hosted on Baseten ([Aircade README](https://github.com/apollo-ullah/Aircade/blob/0b165b4/README.md)). In the team's benchmark of 40 decisions per model, both language models made only valid choices. Astra's median request took 1,836.5 ms against Jev's 322 ms ([Aircade README](https://github.com/apollo-ullah/Aircade/blob/0b165b4/README.md)). Throughout, the model only picks the shot and the native engine handles all physics and contact ([Aircade on Devpost](https://devpost.com/software/aircade-m4n6zv)).

Poddle reached the same goal by a scrappier route. A Swift command-line reader printed one JSON line per sample. A Node bridge relayed those lines over a WebSocket on port 8787 to a three.js browser game. A 9 rad/s threshold, combined with credit for range of motion, separated real swings from flicks. Drawing the paddle 65 ms in the past cut visible jitter from 565% to 9% of a frame step ([Poddle README](https://github.com/danielrltan/poddle/blob/7b0b37ada91f7d079e60a8e16ef844d42831877c/README.md); [Poddle NOTES.md](https://github.com/danielrltan/poddle/blob/7b0b37ada91f7d079e60a8e16ef844d42831877c/NOTES.md)). Its notes also record a trap worth knowing. A command-line Swift binary is silently denied motion access unless an Info.plist with `NSMotionUsageDescription` is embedded in it at link time ([Poddle NOTES.md](https://github.com/danielrltan/poddle/blob/7b0b37ada91f7d079e60a8e16ef844d42831877c/NOTES.md)).

### Rebuilding it on Windows puts an Apple device in the loop

Both Hack the North AirPod projects need macOS 14 or later, and Windows has no official way to read AirPods motion. A Windows 11 developer therefore needs an Apple device to relay the data, or an experimental driver. The cheapest relay is an iPhone app. **Sensor Logger** (free) includes a "headphone" reading in the JSON it sends over HTTP or MQTT (a lightweight messaging protocol). The reading carries the AirPods quaternion, rotation rate, gravity, acceleration and which bud sent it ([Sensor Logger PUSHING.md](https://github.com/tszheichoi/awesome-sensor-logger/blob/main/PUSHING.md)). The free tier sends one batch per second ([Sensor Logger help](https://www.tszheichoi.com/sensorloggerhelp)), which suits logging but not game control. **Kishimen**, an open-source iOS app, sends every full `CMDeviceMotion` sample as one JSON UDP packet to any address and port. It is not on the App Store, so you build it yourself ([Kishimen](https://github.com/takanakahiko/Kishimen)). **SmoothTrack** ($14.99, iOS 26 or later) can feed AirPods Pro head direction into OpenTrack, a head-tracking program, on Windows over UDP port 4242. Its AirPods mode, though, now appears only in old version notes, so check that it still works before paying ([SmoothTrack on the App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/smoothtrack/id1528839485); [SmoothTrack site](https://smoothtrack.app/)). If you write your own OpenTrack sender, each packet is six little-endian doubles, and the angles must be in degrees, not Core Motion's radians ([OpenTrack UDP tracker source](https://github.com/opentrack/opentrack/blob/master/tracker-udp/ftnoir_tracker_udp.cpp)).

You can also write your own reader without a Mac, in two ways. The free Swift Playground app on an iPad with iPadOS 18 or later can build apps that use motion data ([Apple: Swift Playgrounds](https://developer.apple.com/swift-playgrounds/); [HeadphoneMotionTracking.swiftpm](https://github.com/kkebo/HeadphoneMotionTracking.swiftpm)). The open-source tool xtool builds and installs iPhone apps from Windows through WSL. With a free Apple Account it is limited to three apps, which must be re-signed every seven days ([xtool](https://github.com/xtool-org/xtool); [Apple: Compare memberships](https://developer.apple.com/support/compare-memberships/)). Any iPhone app that sends UDP or TCP to the laptop triggers Apple's Local Network permission prompt, and if the user refuses, the stream is silently blocked ([Apple TN3179](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/technotes/tn3179-understanding-local-network-privacy)). The phone-free route uses MagicAAP, a third-party Windows driver that reaches Apple's proprietary Bluetooth channel. On top of it, aap-head-tracker sends OpenTrack packets at about 50 Hz, but it is confirmed only on AirPods Max 2. The official driver is unsigned, so Windows must run in Test Mode, which allows unsigned drivers to load ([aap-head-tracker](https://github.com/FIocker/aap-head-tracker); [MagicAAP](https://magicpods.app/magicaap/)). AirPodsGyro, a project created on November 7, 2026, claims about 100 Hz from AirPods Pro 3 over the PC's own Bluetooth. Its driver loads only with test-signing on and Secure Boot off, and the project is marked paused ([AirPodsGyro](https://github.com/CrossBowKill/AirPodsGyro)). On Android, LibrePods reads the same channel. It outputs only pitch, yaw and two acceleration values, and most phones need root plus Xposed, an Android modding framework ([LibrePods](https://github.com/librepods-org/librepods)). Whatever the route, keep the AirPods microphone out of use. Developers report that motion updates stop when Bluetooth recording is enabled ([Apple Developer Forums 681301](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/681301)).

## Phones are the universal motion sensor for a Windows laptop

Almost every live motion stream that can reach a Windows laptop either starts at a phone or passes through one. The phone's own sensors are also the easiest to read, so the question of which device to use comes down mostly to how the data travels. The table summarizes the routes found in this research. The sections after it explain the limits behind each row.

|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| iPhone | Safari `devicemotion`; Core Motion through Expo Go, Pythonista or Xcode; phyphox, Sensor Logger, ZIG SIM Pro, GyrOSC, SensorLog | Acceleration, rotation rate, attitude | About 60 Hz in the browser; "usually at least 100 Hz" native | HTTPS page plus WebSocket, or UDP, OSC or HTTP from an app | No, except for Xcode-built apps |
| Android phone | Chrome `devicemotion`; SensorManager; SensorServer, SensaGram, HyperIMU, phyphox, Sensor Logger | Acceleration, gyroscope, rotation vector | About 60 Hz in the browser; up to 200 Hz native, more with a permission | WebSocket or UDP over Wi-Fi, or USB through `adb forward` | No |
| AirPods 3, 4, 5, Pro, Max | `CMHeadphoneMotionManager` on an iPhone, iPad or Mac | Attitude, rotation rate, gravity, user acceleration, from one bud at a time | About 25 Hz on iPhone; about 30 to 50 Hz on Mac | iPhone relay app (Sensor Logger, Kishimen, SmoothTrack); experimental MagicAAP driver | No for ready-made apps; a custom app needs a Mac, an iPad or xtool |

### A browser page gets 60 Hz after one tap

The route that needs no installation is a web page. The W3C `devicemotion` event reports `acceleration` and `accelerationIncludingGravity` in m/s², and `rotationRate` in degrees per second ([W3C Device Orientation and Motion](https://www.w3.org/TR/orientation-event/)). Both mobile browser engines deliver it at **60 Hz**. Chromium, the engine behind Chrome, hard-codes its sensor updates at 60 Hz, and WebKit, the engine behind Safari, samples Core Motion every sixtieth of a second on iOS ([Chromium device_sensor_event_pump.h](https://github.com/chromium/chromium/blob/main/third_party/blink/renderer/modules/device_orientation/device_sensor_event_pump.h); [WebKit WebCoreMotionManager.mm](https://github.com/WebKit/WebKit/blob/main/Source/WebCore/platform/ios/WebCoreMotionManager.mm)). The event only fires on secure pages (HTTPS or localhost), so a phone that opens `http://192.168.x.x` on the laptop receives nothing ([Chromium device_motion_event.idl](https://github.com/chromium/chromium/blob/main/third_party/blink/renderer/modules/device_orientation/device_motion_event.idl)). On iPhone the page must call `DeviceMotionEvent.requestPermission()` from a tap ([MDN requestPermission](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/DeviceMotionEvent/requestPermission_static)). This also applies to Chrome, Edge, Firefox and in-app browsers on iOS (unless the host app grants access itself), because Apple requires them all to use WebKit outside the EU and Japan ([App Store Review Guidelines 2.5.6](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/); [WebKit preference defaults](https://github.com/WebKit/WebKit/blob/main/Source/WebKit/Shared/WebPreferencesDefaultValues.cpp)). Chrome added the same method in version 151 or 152. It still allows sensors by default, so the call returns "granted" without a prompt ([chromestatus](https://chromestatus.com/feature/5915984063889408); [Chromium content settings](https://github.com/chromium/chromium/blob/main/components/content_settings/core/browser/content_settings_registry.cc)). The same code therefore works on both platforms.

Three quirks catch real code. First, the event's `interval` field is unusable across browsers. Judging from each engine's source code, it reads 16 in Chrome, about 0.0167 in Safari (seconds, not the spec's milliseconds) and 100 in Firefox, so take timestamps yourself ([Chromium device_motion_event_pump.cc](https://github.com/chromium/chromium/blob/main/third_party/blink/renderer/modules/device_orientation/device_motion_event_pump.cc); [WebKit DeviceMotionClientIOS.mm](https://github.com/WebKit/WebKit/blob/main/Source/WebCore/platform/ios/DeviceMotionClientIOS.mm); [Firefox nsDeviceSensors.cpp](https://github.com/mozilla-firefox/firefox/blob/main/dom/system/nsDeviceSensors.cpp)). Second, both engines read the latest sensor value on a 60 Hz timer, so a late sample shows up as a repeated reading rather than a gap ([Chromium device_sensor_event_pump.cc](https://github.com/chromium/chromium/blob/main/third_party/blink/renderer/modules/device_orientation/device_sensor_event_pump.cc)). Third, iOS has long reported `accelerationIncludingGravity` with the opposite sign to Android ([W3C mailing list, 2014](https://lists.w3.org/Archives/Public/public-geolocation/2014Oct/0022.html)), and WebKit still passes Core Motion's values through unchanged. Calibrate by checking the sign of the vertical axis while the phone lies flat. The newer Generic Sensor API (`Gyroscope`, `AbsoluteOrientationSensor`) does not help. It runs only in Chromium browsers and is capped at the same 60 Hz ([MDN browser-compat-data](https://github.com/mdn/browser-compat-data/blob/main/api/Gyroscope.json); [Chromium sensor_traits.h](https://github.com/chromium/chromium/blob/main/services/device/public/cpp/generic_sensor/sensor_traits.h)).

### Native code raises the ceiling to 100-200 Hz

Native apps get more data, faster. On iOS, `CMMotionManager` provides three motion streams, besides magnetometer data. Raw accelerometer data comes in g. Raw gyroscope data comes in rad/s and "may be biased by other factors such as temperature". Fused device motion combines attitude, bias-corrected rotation rate, gravity and user acceleration. The maximum rate depends on the hardware and "is usually at least 100 Hz" ([Apple: raw gyroscope events](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/coremotion/getting-raw-gyroscope-events); [Apple: processed device motion](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/coremotion/getting-processed-device-motion-data)). Apple asks apps to create only one manager, because several instances can lower the delivered rate ([Apple: CMMotionManager](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/coremotion/cmmotionmanager)). iOS 27.2, released October 26, 2026, added `CMMotionManager.authorizationStatus` ([Apple: authorizationStatus](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/coremotion/cmmotionmanager/authorizationstatus)). Press reports link it to a "Restrict Motion Data" setting aimed at shake-to-open ads for mainland China accounts ([MacRumors](https://macrumors.com/2026/09/23/iphone-setting-shake-off-motion-sensing-ads)). Apple has not documented what it blocks, so code should handle a denied status.

On Android, `SensorManager` provides `TYPE_ACCELEROMETER` in m/s² (gravity included) and `TYPE_GYROSCOPE` in rad/s. Apps targeting Android 12 or later are capped at **200 Hz** unless they declare the `HIGH_SAMPLING_RATE_SENSORS` permission. All motion sensors are rate-limited when the user turns off microphone access. From Android 9, continuous sensors stop delivering data when the app is in the background, so a recording app needs a foreground service ([Android sensors overview](https://developer.android.com/develop/sensors-and-location/sensors/sensors_overview)). Android is the only phone platform whose official tools let a Windows developer build, install and debug native sensor code from start to finish.

On iPhone, Xcode needs a Mac, but besides xtool, two Windows-friendly paths reach native Core Motion. Expo Go, a React Native app runner, runs `expo-sensors` code written on Windows with no paid developer account. Its `Gyroscope` module reports rad/s, while its `DeviceMotion` module reports rotation in deg/s ([Expo Gyroscope](https://docs.expo.dev/versions/latest/sdk/gyroscope/); [Expo DeviceMotion](https://docs.expo.dev/versions/latest/sdk/devicemotion/)). Pythonista 3 ($9.99) runs Python on the phone itself. Its `motion` module gives gravity, user acceleration and attitude, but documents no way to read rotation rate ([Pythonista motion docs](https://omz-software.com/pythonista/docs/ios/motion.html)). Distributing an app beyond your own devices needs the Apple Developer Program, which costs $99 a year ([Apple Developer Program](https://developer.apple.com/programs/enroll/)).

### Free apps already stream phone motion to a laptop

| App | Platform and price | How data leaves the phone | Rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| [phyphox](https://phyphox.org/docs/remote-interface/) | iOS and Android, free and open source | The phone runs an HTTP server, and the laptop requests `/get` for new samples (port 80 on iPhone) | Full sensor rate, fetched in batches |
| [Sensor Logger](https://github.com/tszheichoi/awesome-sensor-logger/blob/main/PUSHING.md) | iOS and Android, free with paid tiers | JSON sent by HTTP POST or MQTT to any URL, including AirPods and Apple Watch readings | One batch per second on the free tier |
| [ZIG SIM Pro](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/zig-sim-pro/id1481556614) | iOS, $3.99 | OSC or JSON over UDP or TCP | 1, 10, 30 or 60 messages per second |
| [GyrOSC](https://www.bitshapesoftware.com/instruments/gyrosc/) | iOS, $0.99 | OSC over UDP | 1 to 120 messages per second |
| [SensorLog](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/sensorlog/id388014573) | iOS and watchOS, $2.99 | TCP or UDP as server or client, or HTTP to a REST API | Up to 100 Hz over TCP or UDP; 20 Hz over HTTP |
| [SensorServer](https://github.com/umer0586/SensorServer) | Android, free and open source | The phone is a WebSocket server, found by mDNS; over USB through `adb forward` | Not documented |
| [SensaGram](https://github.com/UmerCodez/SensaGram) | Android | JSON over UDP to the laptop | Not documented |
| [HyperIMU](https://ianovir.com/works/mobile/hyperimu/hyperimu-help/) | Android, free | CSV over UDP or TCP, or JSON over TCP | Sampling period set by the user |

The table hides one important split: some apps make the phone a server, and others make it a sender. phyphox and SensorServer listen on the phone, so the laptop connects to them. That suits a phone hotspot. On iOS it also avoids the Local Network prompt, because accepting incoming TCP connections is exempt ([Apple TN3179](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/technotes/tn3179-understanding-local-network-privacy)). phyphox lets the laptop pass a time threshold to `/get` and receive only the values after it ([phyphox remote interface](https://phyphox.org/docs/remote-interface/)). Passing the last timestamp received should therefore collect every sample, even with slow polling, though this pattern is untested. Sender apps (Sensor Logger, ZIG SIM, GyrOSC, SensorLog, SensaGram) need the laptop to be reachable and need an inbound rule in Windows Firewall. Sensor Logger is the one that survives Wi-Fi that isolates devices from each other, because it can POST to a public HTTPS tunnel URL ([Sensor Logger PUSHING.md](https://github.com/tszheichoi/awesome-sensor-logger/blob/main/PUSHING.md)). For real-time control, prefer per-message UDP or OSC (GyrOSC sends up to 120 messages a second) over batched HTTP.

### Client isolation and the Windows firewall dictate the network design

Campus and venue networks often block traffic between devices, a setting called client isolation. phyphox warns that "many more professional WIFI setups do not allow for devices to communicate to each other". It recommends a phone hotspot instead, and notes that an iPhone hotspot needs a cellular data connection ([phyphox remote control](https://phyphox.org/remote-control/)). Android adds a cable option. Chrome's USB port forwarding makes the laptop's server appear as `localhost` on the phone, which counts as a secure page, and the connection "doesn't depend on your network configuration" ([Chrome DevTools](https://developer.chrome.com/docs/devtools/remote-debugging/local-server)). `adb forward` does the same for SensorServer.

For a web page, the sturdiest setup is a single server on the laptop that serves both the page and a `/ws` WebSocket, exposed through an HTTPS tunnel. A tunnel is a service that gives a local server a public HTTPS address. The phone then gets a trusted certificate, a secure page and a WebSocket on the same address, and it only needs internet access. Cloudflare's quick tunnels need no account and allow 200 requests in flight at once. Their page does not mention WebSocket support, though Cloudflare Tunnel in general supports it, so test before the event ([Cloudflare quick tunnels](https://developers.cloudflare.com/tunnel/get-started/quick-tunnels/); [Cloudflare Tunnel FAQ](https://developers.cloudflare.com/cloudflare-one/faq/tunnel/)). ngrok's free plan caps HTTP at 20,000 requests a month, which a loop posting 10 times a second would use up in about 33 minutes. Open one WebSocket instead of posting each sample ([ngrok free plan limits](https://ngrok.com/docs/pricing-limits/free-plan-limits)).

Two shortcuts fail on iPhone. Safari blocks an insecure `ws://192.168.x.x` socket opened from an HTTPS page, treating it as mixed content ([WebKit WebSocket.cpp](https://github.com/WebKit/WebKit/blob/main/Source/WebCore/Modules/websockets/WebSocket.cpp)). Chrome on Android allows the same connection to private IP addresses after a Local Network Access prompt ([Chrome Local Network Access guide](https://developer.chrome.com/docs/modern-web-guidance/guides/security/local-network-access)). Secure WebSockets to a laptop with a self-signed certificate are the second risky choice. Cockpit's documentation says Safari rejects them even after the user trusts the certificate ([Cockpit: Safari](https://cockpit-project.org/running/safari)), current WebKit code suggests otherwise, and no first-hand test on iOS 26 or 27 has settled it. Use a tunnel, or install an mkcert root certificate on the iPhone with full trust enabled ([mkcert](https://github.com/FiloSottile/mkcert)).

Finally, Windows itself. When a receiver first opens a port, Windows Defender Firewall prompts. Dismissing that prompt leaves the phone unable to connect, and Windows does not ask again ([PhoneGyro troubleshooting](https://github.com/MrHoustonOff/PhoneGyro/blob/main/docs/troubleshooting.en.md)). Mark the Wi-Fi or hotspot network as Private and add an inbound allow rule with `New-NetFirewallRule` ([Microsoft Learn: firewall rules](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/security/operating-system-security/network-security/windows-firewall/rules); [New-NetFirewallRule](https://learn.microsoft.com/powershell/module/netsecurity/new-netfirewallrule)).

### Dolphin's DSU client turns any stream into a Wii Remote

For a literal Wii Remote on Windows, the Dolphin emulator already understands the cemuhook "DSU" protocol, a standard way to feed controller motion to emulators. A DSU server sends fixed 100-byte UDP packets on port 26760, with accelerometer values in g and gyroscope rates in deg/s ([cemuhook-protocol](https://github.com/v1993/cemuhook-protocol)). You enable Dolphin's DSU client under Controller Settings, Alternate Input Sources, and add the server's address and port. Once you pick its `DSUClient` device for an emulated Wii Remote, the default bindings map those values onto the Wii Remote's accelerometer and MotionPlus gyroscope ([Dolphin wiki: DSU Client](https://wiki.dolphin-emu.org/index.php?title=DSU_Client); [Dolphin WiimoteEmu.cpp](https://github.com/dolphin-emu/dolphin/blob/master/Source/Core/Core/HW/WiimoteEmu/WiimoteEmu.cpp)). The real accelerometer data reaches the game, so the game detects swings and shakes itself, as on a real Wii. Dolphin builds the pointer by integrating the gyroscope. It corrects pitch and roll by blending in 2% of the accelerometer reading, a complementary filter (the help text advises "Consider values between 1% and 3%"). It clamps yaw to a 25° "Total Yaw" and re-zeroes it on a Recenter button, because gravity can never correct yaw drift ([Dolphin IMUCursor.cpp](https://github.com/dolphin-emu/dolphin/blob/master/Source/Core/InputCommon/ControllerEmu/ControlGroup/IMUCursor.cpp)).

Ready-made phone servers exist. **PhoneGyro** (MIT licence) runs the DSU server on the PC and shows a QR code that opens a web page on the phone. An iPhone must first install PhoneGyro's local certificate, because "Safari only exposes gyroscope data over HTTPS". A calibration wizard, which you cannot skip, fixes the axis differences between iOS and Android ([PhoneGyro](https://github.com/MrHoustonOff/PhoneGyro)). **PadForge** does the same with a phone web controller, but it always runs with administrator rights and uses a non-commercial licence ([PadForge](https://github.com/hifihedgehog/PadForge)). The free **DSUController** app ships a ready-made Dolphin Wii Remote profile ([DSUController guides](https://github.com/breeze2/dsu-controller-guides)). I found no ready-made AirPods-to-DSU server. SmoothTrack, OpenTrack and the small OpenTrackDSUServer bridge could in principle be chained, but the chain is untested ([OpenTrackDSUServer](https://github.com/iwubcode/OpenTrackDSUServer)). With a slow head-motion source, lower Dolphin's 2 °/s gyroscope dead zone, which otherwise turns slow head turns into zero ([Dolphin IMUGyroscope.cpp](https://github.com/dolphin-emu/dolphin/blob/master/Source/Core/InputCommon/ControllerEmu/ControlGroup/IMUGyroscope.cpp)). A custom server is small but strict. Dolphin rejects packets with a wrong checksum or length, keeps only the newest packet each time it reads, and expects data to go back to the same IP address and port that each subscription came from ([Dolphin DualShockUDPClient.cpp](https://github.com/dolphin-emu/dolphin/blob/master/Source/Core/InputCommon/ControllerInterface/DualShockUDPClient/DualShockUDPClient.cpp)).

Outside Dolphin, Python's `vgamepad` library still creates a virtual Xbox 360 controller, or a DualShock 4 with motion fields. It does so by installing the ViGEmBus driver, which was retired in 2023 ([vgamepad](https://github.com/yannbouteiller/vgamepad); [ViGEmBus](https://github.com/nefarius/ViGEmBus)). HIDMaestro and VIIPER are maintained alternatives ([HIDMaestro](https://github.com/hifihedgehog/HIDMaestro); [VIIPER](https://github.com/Alia5/VIIPER)). For a pointer alone, moving the Windows cursor is enough, because Dolphin's default simulated Wii pointer follows the mouse ([Dolphin WiimoteEmu.cpp](https://github.com/dolphin-emu/dolphin/blob/master/Source/Core/Core/HW/WiimoteEmu/WiimoteEmu.cpp)). A few lines of Python with pynput can move it ([pynput Windows mouse](https://github.com/moses-palmer/pynput/blob/master/lib/pynput/mouse/_win32.py)). Budget latency carefully. In one study, delays between pressing a button and seeing the result usually went unnoticed below a median threshold of 51 to 90 ms, though some participants noticed delays under 40 ms ([Raaen et al. 2014](https://cms.simula.no/sites/default/files/publications/files/paper_5.pdf)). An iPhone Core Motion controller sending over 5 GHz Wi-Fi measured a mean delay of 70.4 ms ([arXiv 2508.01110](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.01110)). A phone pointer therefore sits right where lag becomes visible. Do not smooth the gyroscope signal to hide jitter: PhoneGyro removed its gyroscope smoothing because "any low-pass filter on angular rate adds delay, and the client integrates it into an overshoot" ([PhoneGyro motion pipeline](https://github.com/MrHoustonOff/PhoneGyro/blob/main/docs/motion-pipeline.en.md)).

## Watches relay through phones, and Fitbit closes November 28

### Apple Watch streams 100 Hz, but only through an iPhone

A watchOS app reads motion through Core Motion's device motion: attitude, bias-corrected rotation rate, gravity and user acceleration. `CMMotionManager` delivers it live at up to **100 Hz**. `CMBatchedSensorManager` delivers **800 Hz accelerometer and 200 Hz device motion** data, but only as "a batch of data per second", and only during "an active HealthKit workout session" ([WWDC23 session 10179](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2023/10179/)). In 2023 Apple named the Series 8 and Ultra as supporting the batched rates, and it has published no list since, so check `isDeviceMotionSupported` on the actual watch ([Apple: CMBatchedSensorManager](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/coremotion/cmbatchedsensormanager)). Build on device motion rather than the raw gyroscope. Developers with Series 3, 6, 7 and 8 watches report `isGyroAvailable` returning false, and an Apple engineer replied that "there is no solution or a workaround" ([Apple Developer Forums 126215](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/126215)). A watch app normally suspends when the user lowers their wrist. Sensors keep running with the wrist down inside an `HKWorkoutSession` or an extended runtime session, such as the one-hour "physical therapy" type that Apple's own example uses for range-of-motion exercises ([Apple: running workout sessions](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/healthkit/running-workout-sessions); [Apple: extended runtime sessions](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/watchkit/using-extended-runtime-sessions)). `CMSensorRecorder` can also log accelerometer data alone at 50 Hz in the background, for the app to collect later ([Apple: recordAccelerometer](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/coremotion/cmsensorrecorder/recordaccelerometer(forduration:))).

Getting the data off the wrist is the hard part. Apple's technical note TN3135 blocks low-level networking for ordinary watch apps: the Network framework, raw TCP and UDP, `URLSessionWebSocketTask` and Bonjour. It also warns that "the simulator always allows low-level networking", a trap for anyone who tests there first ([Apple TN3135](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/technotes/tn3135-low-level-networking-on-watchos)). An Apple Developer Technical Support engineer added that even an ordinary `URLSession` web request to an address on the local network "won't work by default" ([Apple Developer Forums 785846](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/785846)). The practical live path is therefore **watch to iPhone over WatchConnectivity, then iPhone to laptop over Wi-Fi**. `sendMessageData` delivers messages in order while the watch app is reachable, and a workout session keeps it reachable ([Apple: isReachable](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/watchconnectivity/wcsession/isreachable)). An Apple engineer once advised sending "a couple per second" or waiting for each reply before sending more, which in practice means packing several samples into each message ([Apple Developer Forums 9368](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/9368)). I found no recent published latency figure for this hop, so timestamp samples on the watch and measure.

Ready-made apps avoid all of that. **TDInput** ($0.99) is the only app found that is built for live streaming. The iPhone forwards wrist pitch, roll and yaw at 50 Hz, plus rotation rate, acceleration, gravity and heart rate, as OSC over local Wi-Fi, and a workout session keeps the sensors running ([TDInput on the App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/tdinput/id6760799367)). Its listing mentions only Mac receivers, but OSC works on any platform, so a Windows OSC listener such as python-osc should work, though no one has documented that setup. **Sensor Logger** records "wrist motion" (rotation rate, gravity, acceleration and quaternion). In its Phone & Watch mode it streams this through the phone, in one-second batches on the free tier ([Sensor Logger help](https://www.tszheichoi.com/sensorloggerhelp)). **SensorLog** logs all watch sensors at up to 50 Hz in the background and can POST from the watch at up to 10 Hz. It dropped TCP/IP streaming in 2023 "due to changes in the low level networking functions of watchOS" ([SensorLog on the App Store](https://apps.apple.com/gb/app/sensorlog/id388014573)).

A custom watch app, by contrast, needs a Mac. Xcode 27 requires macOS Tahoe 26.6 or later, xtool builds only iOS apps, and Xcode is "the only way" to install your own app on a watch ([Apple: Xcode system requirements](https://developer.apple.com/xcode/system-requirements/); [xtool](https://github.com/xtool-org/xtool); [kenhv.com sideloading guide](https://kenhv.com/blog/sideloading-on-any-apple-product)). A borrowed Mac and a free Apple Account are enough for a hackathon. HealthKit and background modes are available to free accounts on watchOS, with the usual seven-day reinstall ([Apple: watchOS capabilities](https://developer.apple.com/help/account/reference/supported-capabilities-watchos)). The watch itself also needs an iPhone: for watchOS 27, an iPhone 11 or later, or an iPhone SE (2nd generation) or later, running iOS 27 ([Apple Support](https://support.apple.com/en-us/108926)). Since June 2024, a watch app can also read AirPods motion directly, so one app could log head and wrist motion together ([Apple: Core Motion updates](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/updates/coremotion)).

### Fitbit's gyroscope door is shutting

Fitbit's gyroscope was never widely open to developers. Fitbit's SDK guide, which covers only the watches its SDK supports, says "Only the Fitbit Versa and Fitbit Sense contain a gyroscope sensor", even though the original Versa's manual lists no gyroscope ([Fitbit gyroscope guide](https://dev.fitbit.com/build/guides/sensors/gyroscope/); [Versa manual](https://storage.googleapis.com/support-kms-prod/aPbS6cdkChepr3Xoz8DQ3B5hbFEj7Sh0Bo3l)). An earlier version of the guide also listed the Ionic, which has since been recalled, and Google now tells owners to stop using it ([Google Health Help: Ionic recall](https://support.google.com/googlehealth/answer/15118230?hl=en)). Versa Lite, Versa 2 and Versa 3 run apps but have no gyroscope. Sense 2 has a gyroscope ([Google Store: Sense 2](https://store.google.com/us/product/fitbit_sense_2_specs?hl=en-US)), but Fitbit told 9to5Google it has no plans to offer third-party apps on Sense 2 or Versa 4, only clock faces ([9to5Google, 2023](https://9to5google.com/2023/02/17/fitbit-studio/)). The trackers have no app platform. The 2026 Fitbit Air and Fitbit Edge have gyroscopes, but Google lists no SDK or third-party apps for either ([Google Store: Fitbit Air](https://store.google.com/us/product/google_fitbit_air_specs?hl=en-US); [Google Store: Fitbit Edge](https://store.google.com/us/product/google_fitbit_edge_specs?hl=en-US)).

On a Versa or Sense, the SDK's `Gyroscope` reports rad/s, its `Accelerometer` reports m/s² including gravity at a default 100 Hz, and its `OrientationSensor` reports a quaternion. Each is configured with a requested frequency and batch size, and none needs a permission ([Fitbit accelerometer guide](https://dev.fitbit.com/build/guides/sensors/accelerometer/); [Fitbit sensors API](https://dev.fitbit.com/build/reference/device-api/sensors/)). Data leaves the watch only in messages of at most 1,027 bytes or as files through the File Transfer API, sent to a companion script that runs inside the phone's Fitbit app, now called the Google Health app. With the `access_internet` permission, the companion can forward data with `fetch`, which requires HTTPS except to a local-network IP address, or with a WebSocket ([Fitbit messaging guide](https://dev.fitbit.com/build/guides/communications/messaging/); [Fitbit companion fetch](https://dev.fitbit.com/build/reference/companion-api/fetch/)). A community bridge gets around the HTTPS rule by running a Python WebSocket server on the phone itself at `127.0.0.1`, "the only URL we can use without SSL" ([fitbit-stream-bridge](https://github.com/gondwanasoft/fitbit-stream-bridge)).

None of that helps new projects. Google says that "starting on November 28, 2026, you can no longer install or update third-party apps and clocks on Fitbit smartwatches". Apps already installed keep working only until they are deleted or the watch is reset ([Google Health Help](https://support.google.com/googlehealth/answer/14237121?hl=en)). The login endpoints the SDK's command-line tool uses on api.fitbit.com already return HTTP 410 ("Gone"), with a notice that they were "retired on October 30, 2026". Its npm packages have not changed since 2023. Installing an app from a computer through the Fitbit developer bridge therefore almost certainly no longer works. That conclusion comes from reading the tool's code and testing those endpoints, not from running the tool ([developer-bridge environments.json](https://github.com/Google-Health-API/developer-bridge/blob/master/packages/sdk-cli/src/auth/environments.json); [npm @fitbit/sdk-cli](https://www.npmjs.com/package/@fitbit/sdk-cli)). The cloud is no substitute. None of the Google Health API's 44 data types is an accelerometer, gyroscope or orientation stream. The finest motion data is steps and distance per minute, and heart rate goes down to one second ([Google Health API data types](https://developers.google.com/health/data-types)).

Owners who want Fitbit-like wrist data have three real options. The first is a Pixel Watch or Galaxy Watch4 or later running Wear OS. There, ordinary Android `SensorManager` code reads the gyroscope at up to 200 Hz ([Android sensors overview](https://developer.android.com/develop/sensors-and-location/sensors/sensors_overview)) and can send HTTP, TCP or UDP straight over Wi-Fi ([Wear OS network access](https://developer.android.com/training/wearables/data/network-access)). Neither watch works with an iPhone ([Pixel Watch Help](https://support.google.com/googlepixelwatch/answer/12652073?hl=en); [Samsung support](https://www.samsung.com/us/support/answer/ANS10004668/)). The second is a Garmin running Connect IQ, whose sensor listener returns batches of gyroscope data in deg/s, at up to 100 Hz on supported models since SDK 6.3.0, relayed over Bluetooth through the phone ([Garmin Connect IQ Sensor API](https://developer.garmin.com/connect-iq/api-docs/Toybox/Sensor.html); [Connect IQ 6.3.0 release](https://forums.garmin.com/developer/connect-iq/b/news-announcements/posts/connect-iq-6-3-0-sdk-release)). The third is an Apple Watch, through the iPhone path above.

## A 60 Hz browser walk test is buildable but unvalidated for screening

The closest published model for the BioHacks test is WatchALS. In it, 19 people with ALS and 23 controls did weekly home tasks on an iPhone and Apple Watch for 24 weeks. Its 60-second walk computed gait speed simply as distance covered divided by walking time. That measure showed **test-retest ICC 0.93** in ALS and correlated 0.83 with the gross-motor section of the self-reported ALSFRS, the standard ALS functional rating scale. The combined task features separated ALS from controls with AUCs of 0.75 to 0.92, where 0.5 is chance and 1.0 is perfect separation ([WatchALS, JMIR Formative Research 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13423962/)). Its limits matter as much: "many participants with ALS were either unable to complete the walking task or changed the assistive devices they used". Every cohort in this literature had already been diagnosed. A systematic review of 20 sensor studies in motor neurone disease rated the risk of bias high ([Beswick et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00415-022-11312-7)). This research found no study validating a phone, watch or earbud test for detecting undiagnosed ALS or for deciding referrals.

Where the sensor sits decides how accurate it is. A pocketed iPhone sampling at 100 Hz matched a GAITRite pressure walkway on stride time with a mean error of 16.9 ms (r² = .98) in 14 healthy young adults ([Manor et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5811655/)). Phones strapped to the body or a belt were the most reliable, with an ICC of at least 0.90, against at least 0.69 for a bag, hand or pocket ([Silsupadol et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.gaitpost.2017.09.030)). Phone estimates of step variability and asymmetry, however, "generally showed poor validity and reliability" ([Rashid et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.3390/s22010124)). Wrist-worn sensors detect walking less reliably than sensors on the lower back ([Kluge et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.2196/50035)). Consumer step counters undercount in ALS: a Fitbit Sense read about 14% below a research-grade accelerometer in people with ALS ([Jewett et al. 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13471975/)). AirPods are the weakest source: about 25 Hz on an iPhone, from one bud at a time, with dropped data, and this research found no study validating their stride timing.

An unpublished analysis run for this report on the public PhysioNet gait database shows why a 60 Hz browser is not the bottleneck. The database was recorded with force sensors under the feet, not phones. Stride-time variability is measured as the coefficient of variation (CV), the spread of stride times relative to their average. The median ALS record had a CV of 4.86%, against 2.53% for controls (AUC 0.84), though the groups are not age-matched. Rounding heel strikes to a clean 60 Hz grid barely changed the separation (AUC 0.83). A real browser's timer polling can add up to one more frame (about 17 ms) of timing error, of unmeasured typical size. Adding 17 ms of random timing error to each heel strike cut it to about 0.79. Estimates from only 10 strides came out 26% too low, against 5% too low at 50 strides ([PhysioNet gaitndd](https://physionet.org/content/gaitndd/1.0.0/)). Step-detection error and stride count are the limits, not the sampling rate. A variability score therefore needs about 50 clean strides, roughly 60 to 90 seconds of straight walking. It should be compared with the user's own baseline, not with cut-offs from foot-sensor studies.

The build follows directly. Serve the test over HTTPS through a tunnel. Have a Start button call `requestPermission()` and request a Screen Wake Lock, and keep the screen on, because motion events fire only while the page is visible ([W3C](https://www.w3.org/TR/orientation-event/); [MDN Screen Wake Lock](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/Screen_Wake_Lock_API)). Clip the phone at the waist or lower back. Time the walk over a measured course, so gait speed needs no estimate of step length. Find steps by picking peaks in smoothed vertical acceleration, and cross-check them with autocorrelation as validated trunk-sensor methods do ([Moe-Nilssen and Helbostad 2004](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0021-9290(03)00233-1)). Where the disease starts shapes how much the arms are used ([Straczkiewicz et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ebiom.2024.105036)), so add WatchALS's 20-second alternating two-finger tapping test for each hand. A native iOS app could add ResearchKit's open-source Timed Walk task ([ResearchKit](https://github.com/ResearchKit/ResearchKit)). HealthKit's passive Walking Steadiness builds up only after weeks of carrying the phone ([Apple: appleWalkingSteadiness](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/healthkit/hkquantitytypeidentifier/applewalkingsteadiness)). Apple's `CMMovementDisorderManager` is off-limits: it targets Parkinsonian tremor, needs an Apple entitlement, and Apple restricts it to "patients clinically diagnosed with a movement disorder" ([Apple: CMMovementDisorderManager](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/coremotion/cmmovementdisordermanager)). The honest pitch to judges is a test modelled on research tasks used in ALS, not one validated for ALS screening.

## Conclusion

Aircade shows that getting at the sensor was never the hard part: Apple's API is public, and the decisive "hack" was a setting. The engineering went into living with a slow, bursty stream from one bud at a time. The team used freshness checks, geometric sweeps instead of gesture classifiers, and a dwell click in place of a button. The same lesson appears in the gait simulation: timing quality limits motion data more than sample rate does. Errors in detecting each step and too few strides weaken the ALS signal far more than dropping from native rates to a browser's 60 Hz. That changes the priorities for the BioHacks build. Spend effort on where the phone sits, clean step detection and enough strides, not on reaching 200 Hz.

For a Windows developer, the deciding constraint is the network path, not the device. One pipeline could serve both of your goals: an HTTPS page served from the laptop through a tunnel, streaming over a single WebSocket. That pipeline is the walking test. With a small DSU bridge added, it would also rebuild Aircade's Wii-style play on Windows, with a phone in the hand instead of an AirPod. An AirPod version adds only one piece of hardware, an iPhone running a relay app, rather than a Mac. Fitbit has no place in that plan, because its developer platform closes in three weeks.
